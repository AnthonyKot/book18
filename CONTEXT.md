# Book 18 — CONTEXT

**Working title: *The Second Engine*.** Seven evidence-led essays for an experienced
software developer deciding whether one of seven modern technical opportunities can become
a second, scalable business before 2030.

This is an investment book, not a prediction that software employment will disappear. The
reader has one good career, up to EUR 100,000 available, twelve to twenty-four months to
learn, Dutch at A2 with a path to B1/B2, a base in Zaandam, and willingness to travel and do
physical work. The question is which option earns the right to consume time and capital.

Precedence: this file -> `AGENT.md` -> essay contracts (section 6) -> `TEMPLATE.md` -> prose.

## 1. Thesis

The mistake is to choose between "modern but still software" and "AI-resistant but an old
Dutch trade." A better second engine sits at an interface: a new EU obligation or industrial
technology, a buyer with an expensive consequence, and a delivery model that begins with
expert service but can become recurring, teachable, and productised.

The candidates are not recommendations. Each is a hypothesis subjected to the same
sequence inherited from Book 4, *The Going Concern*:

**demand -> payment -> economics -> operations -> resilience**

An essay passes only if it can identify all five. A large market is not demand; a legal duty
is not a budget; revenue is not owner income; automation is not scalability; and novelty is
not protection from competition.

## 2. Reader and fixed assumptions

- About forty, ten-plus years in enterprise full-stack development.
- Dutch passport; based in Zaandam; flexible across the Netherlands and Benelux.
- Dutch A2, potentially B1/B2 in one to two years. The primary business must not require
  the founder personally to win and execute Dutch-language local field work.
- Up to EUR 100,000 investable, excluding ordinary living costs unless an essay says otherwise.
- Target: at least EUR 100,000 annual owner income within a plausible three-to-five-year path.
- Preference: a company that can outgrow founder-hours, not merely premium self-employment.

## 3. Economic gate

Every essay carries a bottom-up model. The default target is EUR 100,000 owner compensation
before personal tax, after operating expenses, with enough retained cash that the company is
not emptied to pay its owner. Figures must separate:

- revenue;
- pass-through hardware or subcontracting;
- gross margin;
- operating costs;
- owner labour at replacement cost;
- recurring revenue;
- working capital and capital expenditure.

Illustrative arithmetic is labelled as a required design or validation hurdle, never reported
as a market fact. Market prices require sources or live buyer evidence.

## 4. Scalability test

"Later a platform" is not evidence. Each essay must say what concretely changes between the
first and twentieth customer. Score the option against four tests:

1. **Repeatable acquisition:** the twentieth buyer is reached through a channel that does not
   depend solely on the founder's personal network.
2. **Teachable delivery:** another trained person can perform a majority of delivery from a
   written playbook.
3. **Recurring value:** some work recurs because the customer's state changes, not because an
   annual report was artificially invented.
4. **Accumulating asset:** integrations, data history, installed equipment, certification,
   workflow or distribution becomes more valuable with use.

An expert practice is a legitimate outcome, but it must not be called a scalable company.

## 5. Evidence and honesty rules

- Current law, deadlines, standards and official programmes: primary sources first (EUR-Lex,
  European Commission, Dutch government, regulators, standards bodies).
- Demand: buyer behaviour, enforcement records, procurement, company filings, trade data or
  attributable practitioner evidence. A regulation alone does not prove willingness to pay.
- Competition: name existing vendors and the substitute, including internal implementation.
- No total-addressable-market theatre. A percentage of a global market is not a sales plan.
- No numbers from memory. Every external factual claim in HTML carries `<!-- CHECK: id -->`
  and a row in `checks/claims/NN.tsv`.
- Distinguish enacted law from proposals, deadlines from expected dates, and legal obligation
  from practical enforcement.
- Each essay must make the strongest case against itself. If the case cannot be answered,
  the verdict is DO NOT ENTER or PARTNER/ACQUIRE, not optimism.
- Sources used by the prose are indexed in `resources/sources/NN/SOURCES.md`, with URL,
  publisher, date accessed, and the claim supported.

## 6. Essay contracts

### 1. Connected Product Compliance Operations

Test a managed operation combining the Cyber Resilience Act and Data Act for European
connected-machinery and industrial-product manufacturers, with a later evidence/workflow
platform. The essay must define one initial customer segment, identify the exact recurring
state changes, distinguish legal interpretation from technical implementation, and test
whether a small manufacturer will buy an external operating function rather than a one-off
consultant or generic GRC tool.

### 2. White-label Product Security for Engineering Consultancies

Test a B2B2B model in which CE advisers, embedded-development firms, OT-security companies or
machine integrators keep the customer relationship and resell a specialist product-security
back office. The essay must confront channel conflict, margin sharing, trust, professional
liability, partner enablement, and the risk that the partner learns the work and removes the
supplier.

### 3. A Narrow Machine-vision Product for Food or Packaging

Do not study "machine vision" in general. Select and justify a narrow recurring defect or
inspection job in food/packaging for which successive installations could become materially
similar. Include lighting, optics, mechanics, PLC/reject integration, validation, cleaning and
food-environment constraints, false accepts/rejects, support, and how Dutch integrators become
channels rather than competitors.

### 4. White-label Industrial Asset Intelligence

Test a condition-monitoring/data layer sold through existing maintenance companies. The local
maintenance firm owns measurement routes, repairs and Dutch customer relationships; the new
company provides ingestion, asset history, analysis workflow, reporting and specialist review.
Confront incumbent platforms, data quality, diagnostic liability, who owns the customer and
data, the need for labelled failures, and whether the software layer has enough value to retain
margin.

### 5. Digital Product Passport / Battery-passport Integration

Choose a narrow initial supply chain, preferably one with a dated obligation, and test an
integration/managed-data business rather than generic DPP consulting. Separate the Battery
Regulation's live requirements from the broader ESPR rollout and standards still being made.
Confront platform incumbents, interoperability, identifier/data-carrier requirements, supplier
data collection, access control, data verification, and the danger of building before delegated
acts and customer budgets settle.

### 6. Grid-congestion Capacity Control for Business Parks

Test a control-and-evidence layer for Dutch "energy hubs": groups of neighbouring businesses
that share or cap grid capacity because the regional grid operator cannot offer a new or larger
connection. The essay must establish from primary sources what contract forms the grid
operators and the regulator actually offer today (non-firm, time-bound, group or capacity-
limiting transport agreements) and their dates, and must not present a proposal as a live
product. Name the buyer (a site whose investment is blocked, not a sustainability officer), the
channel (installers and energy consultants who hold the Dutch-language relationship), the
incumbents (Sympower, Withthegrid, Scholt Energy, Enervalis and the grid operators' own hub
programmes), the liability when a control system fails and a contracted limit is breached, and
whether the software layer keeps margin once the installer owns the hardware and the site.

### 7. EUDI Wallet Acceptance for Regulated Onboarding

Test a verifier-as-a-service plus relying-party registration operation for mid-size
organisations that will be obliged, or commercially pushed, to accept European Digital Identity
Wallet credentials. The essay must pin the obligations to Regulation (EU) 2024/1183 and its
implementing acts: which relying parties are obliged, from when, and what the Dutch NL Wallet
programme has actually shipped. Separate obligation from budget. Name the target buyer (housing
corporations, pre-employment screeners, notaries, rental and sharing platforms; not banks or
telecoms with identity teams), the incumbents (Signicat, Datakeeper, Yivi, Ver.iD, Sphereon),
the risk that wallet timelines slip, and the risk that large verifiers bundle acceptance for
free. This essay is the book's test of its own thesis: pure software at a dated interface.
It must say plainly whether the interface makes it more than "still software".

## 7. Common verdict

Each essay ends with exactly one provisional verdict:

- **TEST NOW** — evidence supports spending a bounded amount on paid discovery.
- **TEST THROUGH A PARTNER** — plausible only with a named channel or domain partner.
- **WATCH** — timing or standards are too unsettled for material investment.
- **ACQUIRE, DO NOT BUILD** — the capability is attractive but greenfield entry is structurally weak.
- **DO NOT ENTER** — no credible route under the reader's constraints.

The verdict is provisional until customer payment exists.

## 8. Book shape

Seven essays, 2,400–3,600 words each. The landing page carries a comparison table and explains
how to read the essays; it is not a sixth essay. The method page explains evidence, assumptions,
and how Book 4's framework was adapted. Essay order follows the current shortlist, not a claimed
final ranking.
