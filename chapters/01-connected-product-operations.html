Essay 1 of 7 · Connected Product Compliance Operations

The law creates a queue, not a customer

A Dutch machine builder is entering a period in which two obligations attach to one connected product: keep its software secure through a long operating life, and make defined product data accessible on defined terms. The attractive business is not another readiness report. It is the small operating team that keeps product, component, vulnerability, evidence and data-access records true after the report is filed. The fatal possibility is simpler: manufacturers may buy a lawyer, an assessor and a software licence once, then give the recurring queue to existing quality and engineering staff. The first investment decision is therefore not whether the regulation is important. It is whether one narrow manufacturer will pay an outsider to own the queue.

The proposition

The first customer is a Netherlands- or Belgium-based maker of connected food-processing or packaging machinery with its own firmware or control software, no dedicated product-security operations team, and several product variants still supported in the field. The economic buyer is the engineering director or COO; quality is the internal sponsor. The purchase event is a dated product release, a customer security questionnaire, or a management review that discovers nobody can demonstrate who owns vulnerability intake and data-access requests.

The initial deliverable is a four-week, fixed-scope product operations baseline for one product family. It produces a product-and-version register, an SBOM intake path, a vulnerability triage queue, an incident-notification rehearsal, a support-period record, an evidence index, a map of generated and retrievable product data, and a tested user-request path. The continuation is a managed weekly queue with a named customer owner: changes enter, decisions are assigned, evidence is linked, deadlines are rehearsed, and stale items are escalated.

This company refuses to be the customer's law firm, notified body, penetration-test laboratory, generic vCISO or system integrator. Counsel decides scope, contract language, trade-secret grounds and contested interpretations. An assessor decides whether evidence supports conformity. The manufacturer decides product risk and accepts residual risk. The operation turns those decisions into tickets, artefacts, tests and an audit trail; it can implement an SBOM pipeline or data-export endpoint, but it does not sell its implementation as legal approval.

The substitute is not “do nothing.” It is a one-off CRA/CE adviser plus Jira, SharePoint, an SBOM scanner and employees who add the work to quality and engineering. The operating assumption is that a plausible buyer can assemble that bundle from tools and staff it already has. The managed operation wins only if it closes work across those tools more reliably than an internal coordinator, not because its dashboard is prettier.

What changed

The Cyber Resilience Act applies broadly to products with digital elements made available on the EU market when their intended or reasonably foreseeable use includes a direct or indirect logical or physical data connection. It covers design, development and production requirements, vulnerability-handling processes and market surveillance; machinery can be subject both to the CRA and the Machinery Regulation.

The first operational deadline is immediate. From 11 September 2026 manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents. An early warning is due within 24 hours, a fuller notification within 72 hours, and the vulnerability final report no later than fourteen days after a corrective or mitigating measure is available. The main CRA obligations apply from 11 December 2027. ENISA says its Single Reporting Platform is the tool manufacturers and CSIRTs will use from that first date.

The longer-lived workload matters more than the filing. A manufacturer must document relevant cybersecurity aspects and vulnerabilities, keep the risk assessment current where appropriate, operate coordinated vulnerability disclosure, and handle vulnerabilities through a support period normally at least five years. The Act specifically notes that industrial products are often used much longer; technical documentation must be continuously updated where appropriate during the support period. This is the recurring state: product releases alter components; suppliers disclose faults; new vulnerabilities change exploitability; field configurations change exposure; a fix changes evidence; a customer report changes the incident record; support clocks expire.

The Data Act creates a different queue. Its connected-product definition covers an item that obtains, generates or collects use or environmental data and can communicate product data, where storing or transmitting data for someone other than the user is not the primary function. The Commission explicitly gives robots and industrial machinery as examples. Article 3 requires connected products and related services to make product data, related-service data and necessary metadata accessible by default, easily, securely, free of charge and in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format; pre-contract information must describe the data and access route. Where data are not directly accessible, Article 4 requires the data holder to provide readily available data on a simple electronic request where feasible.

The Data Act has applied generally since 12 September 2025, but the Article 3(1) design obligation applies to connected products and related services placed on the market after 12 September 2026. Trade secrets do not create a casual blanket exemption: the Act sets out confidentiality measures, written substantiation and notifications to the competent authority when data are withheld or suspended. That produces more state changes: a new sensor or firmware build alters the data catalogue; a contract changes permitted use; a user or third party requests access; security or trade-secret controls change the release decision; an interface or retention rule changes.

These laws overlap around product identity, versions, ownership and change control, but they do not become one discipline. CRA interpretation belongs with product-security and conformity specialists. Data Act interpretation belongs with counsel and commercial/product owners. Technical implementation can share an evidence graph and release gates. Selling “combined compliance” without that separation invites bad legal advice and confuses two budgets.

Regulation proves workload, not paid demand. The best directly relevant field evidence found here is a 2024 qualitative survey of twelve Northern European organisations: seven large industrial-equipment manufacturers and five consultancies or partners, recruited partly through a compliance-automation research project. All surveyed manufacturers already had an IEC 62443-4-1-aligned secure-development lifecycle, so they are not the proposed small customer. Respondents described ownership, secure-development enforcement, update management, expertise and 24-hour notification as difficult; they found organisational and procedural issues harder than many technical controls. Yet half expected CRA cost increases below 20%, a quarter expected no cost impact, and half expected employee roles to remain unchanged. The authors explicitly suggest firms may use existing resources. That is evidence of pain and a warning against assuming a budget. No source here shows a small industrial OEM paying an external operator each month.

The first sale

Build a list of ten prospect types, not a mailing list of everyone with an Ethernet port: packaging-line OEM; food-processing machine builder; industrial-weighing maker; warehouse-equipment OEM; water-treatment skid maker; specialist robot cell producer; machine-vision equipment maker; connected pump or compressor maker; retrofit gateway producer; and private-label industrial controller vendor. Each must ship its own branded product, control at least part of the software release, and lack a visible product-security operations team. Importers and distributors are a later segment because their duties and leverage differ.

Reach them through three routes: English-language product-security roundtables with FME/Agoria-type industry groups; introductions from CE/machinery-safety advisers and embedded-development firms; and direct, product-specific notes to engineering or quality leaders. The note names one observable trigger—an approaching release, a public vulnerability contact, a customer data promise—not “the CRA market.” Dutch A2 is insufficient for trust-heavy cold selling into small local factories. The operating hypothesis therefore requires English-capable export manufacturers and a bilingual partner for Dutch workshops and manuals. If the first ten insist that commercial and technical work happen in Dutch, this route is not founder-led.

The sale is a €7,500 baseline, paid, for one family and capped at four weeks. It includes one incident tabletop and one real data-export/request test; it does not promise conformity. At closeout, the buyer receives the artefacts even if it declines the retainer. The continuation offer is €3,000 per month for weekly triage, monthly evidence review and quarterly drills, with implementation projects separately scoped. These are required-price hypotheses, not observed market prices.

The signer should be the person who can assign engineering and quality time. A security enthusiast without that authority cannot buy an operating function because every closed item needs internal judgement or code. “Why now” is the reporting process due in days and the need to learn it before an actual incident, but urgency earns a meeting, not a purchase order. The diagnostic is designed to reveal whether the buyer values closure or merely wants a gap report.

Year one fails the owner-income gate and should: three pilot clients cannot pay a senior founder and build the method. The mature case passes only if three delivery engineers can support twenty customers while the founder moves to sales, escalation and product. That is roughly seven customers per delivery engineer, a capacity assumption to test with time logs. If each customer consumes a day a week, the model collapses into a consultancy.

Recurring revenue is €720,000 in the mature model, 86% of revenue. It is earned by changing customer state, not an invented annual report. Gross margin pays delivery staff and software before corporate overhead. The €100,000 founder line is owner compensation before personal tax at replacement cost; the €310,000 surplus is not salary and should mostly remain available for hiring, tax and product investment.

Working capital is the constraint hidden by annual arithmetic. A three-person delivery payroll plus software and tax can consume about €90,000 before receivables catch up in this model. Do not hire that team against unsigned pipeline. Bill diagnostics 50% in advance, retainers monthly in advance, keep customer-paid laboratory work out of the books, and build the payroll reserve from retained pilot cash. The initial €100,000 is enough only if growth follows receipts.

From service to company

Customer one is founder work. The asset is not software; it is the first complete state model: product, version, component, vulnerability, decision, evidence, data field, request, owner and deadline. Every action records input, decision authority, output and elapsed time. The founder learns where legal judgement ends and implementation begins.

Customer five uses one canonical playbook with product-specific adapters. A trained analyst runs intake, evidence checks and meeting preparation; an engineer handles pipelines and export tests; the founder handles risk escalation. At least 60% of routine hours must be performed without the founder, and every customer must use the same weekly queue and severity rules. Acquisition begins to repeat through two adviser or embedded-firm channels, not five founder friendships.

Customer twenty has a multi-tenant workflow product only where reuse is real: immutable decision history, release evidence, supplier/component links, deadline timers, role-based access and exportable technical-file indexes. Customer code, risk acceptance and legal opinions stay outside or behind strict tenant boundaries. Integrations into Git, issue tracking and document stores are the accumulating asset; so are elapsed-time benchmarks and playbooks. The system does not decide whether a product is legally in scope or whether disclosure would cause serious economic damage.

The four scalability tests are therefore explicit. Acquisition repeats only if two channels each create several paid baselines a year. Delivery is teachable only if analysts close most queue items from written procedures. Value recurs only while releases, vulnerabilities, requests and support states change. The asset accumulates only if integrations and evidence history reduce the next customer's labour. Fail any two and the honest outcome is a good expert practice, not a scalable company.

The incumbent gets a turn

ONEKEY already markets firmware/SBOM analysis, vulnerability monitoring, impact assessment and a compliance workflow for manufacturers, combined with consulting. Cybellum offers a product-security platform spanning SBOMs, risk, vulnerabilities, incident response and regulatory evidence. The EU-funded CRACoWi project states a value of €8.44 million and a term through August 2027; its wizard is intended to help SMEs, manufacturers, importers and distributors automate CRA assessments and documentation. These are not distant category competitors. They can add service, partner with assessors, lower prices or become the customer's system of record.

Generic substitutes are stronger than their product pages suggest. A competent quality manager can join an SBOM scanner, Jira, SharePoint, counsel and an annual penetration test. An embedded supplier can add the work to an existing development retainer. A CE adviser can own the evidence map. The manufacturer may reasonably prefer internal risk acceptance and customer communication because outsourcing adds another sensitive repository and another party during a 24-hour incident.

Standards will also move the boundary. CEN and CENELEC's 2026 programme says horizontal CRA drafts, vulnerability-handling work and industrial vertical profiles are still being advanced, with key work scheduled for the end of 2026. Clearer standards help delivery but commoditise the map. The operation must own reliable closure across tools, not proprietary interpretations that disappear when guidance improves.

The response is deliberately narrow: serve long-lived industrial products where firmware extraction, field variants, intermittent connectivity, supplier images and safe update windows make an ordinary SaaS workflow incomplete. Integrate ONEKEY, Cybellum or open tools when they fit. Never build an SBOM scanner merely to avoid paying a vendor. The defensible margin, if any, sits in cross-functional operation and accumulated customer context.

The capable failure

The company executes well. It recruits industrial-security engineers, builds disciplined integrations, passes customer security reviews and closes queues on time. By late 2027 the first manufacturers have finished their readiness projects. Then the work separates. Quality retains conformity evidence; engineering buys a product-security platform; counsel owns Data Act contracts; a junior internal product-security coordinator runs the weekly meeting. The external operation is renewed only for incident overflow.

Nothing failed operationally. The law created durable tasks but not a durable outsourcing boundary. Better standards reduced interpretation work, product tools automated the mechanical queue, and the remaining decisions were precisely those the manufacturer could not delegate. Revenue settles into diagnostics and occasional remediation. Founder expertise remains valuable, but the twentieth customer requires the same senior attention as the first. This is the strongest objection: capable delivery discovers a finite transition project, not a recurring company.

The first ninety days

  1. Days 1–15: choose only connected food/packaging machinery OEMs and screen twenty accounts for own-brand products, controlled software releases, field support and no dedicated product-security operation. Secure one product-security lawyer and one machinery/CE adviser who will review boundaries, not decorate the website.
  2. Days 16–30: conduct ten 45-minute workflow interviews with engineering, quality and product leaders. Reconstruct the last real release, vulnerability, customer security request and product-data request. Ask who did the work, hours lost, deadline, budget owner and which supplier was paid. Do not pitch until the reconstruction is complete.
  3. Days 31–45: sell the €7,500 baseline to all qualified interviewees. Offer the same scope and price. Maximum total pre-evidence spend is €5,000 for counsel review, insurance advice, travel and a disposable prototype; no platform build, certification or employee.
  4. Days 46–75: deliver at most two paid baselines in the customer's existing tools. Time every task and mark each decision as legal, manufacturer risk, technical implementation or routine operation. Run the incident clock and one data-access path with real product evidence.
  5. Days 76–90: ask each buyer to sign a six-month managed-operation pilot at €3,000 a month, billed monthly in advance. The pass rule is two paid baselines from ten qualified offers and at least one retainer signed, with 60% of forecast recurring work classed as analyst/engineer work rather than founder judgement. The kill rule is no paid baseline after ten qualified offers, or two baselines that expose less than one day a month of recurring external work.

Verdict

TEST NOW

The assumption most able to reverse it is that a small connected-machinery manufacturer will pay an external team every month to close the operating queue after the readiness project ends.
