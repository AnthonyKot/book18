Essay 1 of 7 · Connected Product Compliance Operations

The law creates a queue, not a customer

A Dutch machine builder is entering a period in which two obligations attach to one connected product: secure its software through a long operating life, and make defined product data accessible on defined terms. The attractive business is not another readiness report. It is a small team that keeps the product-security record true after filing. Data-access work shares inputs but has a different buyer. The fatal possibility is simpler: manufacturers may buy advice and software once, then give both queues to existing staff. The investment decision is whether one narrow manufacturer will pay an outsider to operate the CRA queue and buy Data Act implementation separately.

The proposition

The first customer is a Netherlands- or Belgium-based maker of connected food-processing or packaging machinery. It owns firmware or control software, lacks a dedicated product-security operations team, and supports several product variants in the field. The CRA economic buyer is the engineering director. This person can assign engineering and quality work and owns the release decision. The purchase event is a dated release, a customer security questionnaire, or a management review that finds no owner for vulnerability intake.

The initial deliverable is a four-week, fixed-scope CRA operations baseline for one product family. It produces a product-and-version register, an SBOM intake path, a vulnerability queue, an incident rehearsal, a support-period record and an evidence index. The continuation is a managed weekly queue with a named customer owner. Changes enter, decisions are assigned, evidence is linked, deadlines are rehearsed, and stale items are escalated.

The Data Act is a separate module, not a bundled retainer. Its buyer is the product director accountable for commercial data promises, technical access and commissioning counsel. The module maps generated and retrievable data, records approved use and tests one user-request path. It can reuse product identities and evidence links from the CRA baseline without pretending that the budgets or decision rights are shared.

This company refuses to be the customer's law firm, notified body, penetration-test laboratory, generic vCISO or system integrator. Counsel decides scope, contract language, trade-secret grounds and contested interpretations. An assessor decides whether evidence supports conformity. The manufacturer decides product risk and accepts residual risk. The operation turns those decisions into tickets, artefacts, tests and an audit trail; it can implement an SBOM pipeline or data-export endpoint, but it does not sell its implementation as legal approval.

The substitute is not “do nothing.” It is a one-off CRA/CE adviser plus Jira, SharePoint, an SBOM scanner and employees who add the work to quality and engineering. The operating assumption is that a plausible buyer can assemble that bundle from tools and staff it already has. The managed operation wins only if it closes work across those tools more reliably than an internal coordinator, not because its dashboard is prettier.

What changed

The Cyber Resilience Act applies broadly to products with digital elements made available on the EU market when their intended or reasonably foreseeable use includes a direct or indirect logical or physical data connection. It covers design, development and production requirements, vulnerability-handling processes and market surveillance; machinery can be subject both to the CRA and the Machinery Regulation.

CRA reporting starts on 11 September 2026. From that date, manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents. An early warning is due within 24 hours and a fuller notification within 72 hours. The vulnerability final report follows no later than fourteen days after a corrective or mitigating measure becomes available. The main CRA obligations apply from 11 December 2027. ENISA says its Single Reporting Platform is the tool manufacturers and CSIRTs will use from 11 September 2026.

The longer-lived workload matters more than the filing. A manufacturer must document relevant cybersecurity aspects and vulnerabilities, keep the risk assessment current where appropriate, operate coordinated vulnerability disclosure, and handle vulnerabilities through a support period normally at least five years. The Act specifically notes that industrial products are often used much longer; technical documentation must be continuously updated where appropriate during the support period. This is the recurring state: product releases alter components; suppliers disclose faults; new vulnerabilities change exploitability; field configurations change exposure; a fix changes evidence; a customer report changes the incident record; support clocks expire.

The Data Act creates a different queue. Its connected-product definition covers an item that obtains, generates or collects use or environmental data and can communicate product data. Storing or transmitting data for someone other than the user cannot be its primary function. The Commission gives robots and industrial machines as examples. Article 3 requires accessible product data, related-service data and necessary metadata by default. Access must be easy, secure, free and in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format. Pre-contract information must describe the data and access route. Where data are not directly accessible, Article 4 requires the data holder to provide readily available data on a simple electronic request where feasible.

The Data Act has applied generally since 12 September 2025. Its Article 3(1) design obligation covers connected products and related services placed on the market after 12 September 2026. Trade secrets do not create a casual blanket exemption. The Act sets out confidentiality measures, written substantiation and notifications to the competent authority when data are withheld or suspended. That produces more state changes. A new sensor or firmware build alters the data catalogue. A contract changes permitted use, while a user or third party may request access. Security or trade-secret controls change the release decision. An interface or retention rule can also change.

These laws overlap around product identity, versions, ownership and change control, but they do not become one discipline. CRA interpretation belongs with product-security and conformity specialists. Data Act interpretation belongs with counsel and commercial/product owners. Technical implementation can share an evidence graph and release gates. Selling “combined compliance” without that separation invites bad legal advice and confuses two budgets.

Regulation proves workload, not paid demand. The best directly relevant field evidence found here is a 2024 qualitative survey of twelve Northern European organisations: seven large industrial-equipment manufacturers and five consultancies or partners, recruited partly through a compliance-automation research project. All surveyed manufacturers already had an IEC 62443-4-1-aligned secure-development lifecycle, so they are not the proposed small customer. Respondents described ownership, secure-development enforcement, update management, expertise and 24-hour notification as difficult; they found organisational and procedural issues harder than many technical controls. Yet half expected CRA cost increases below 20%, a quarter expected no cost impact, and half expected employee roles to remain unchanged. The authors explicitly suggest firms may use existing resources. That is evidence of pain and a warning against assuming a budget. No source here shows a small industrial OEM paying an external operator each month.

The first sale

Build ten prospect types inside food and packaging machinery, not a mailing list of every connected-device maker. Include packaging-line, tray-sealing, filling, dosing, slicing, weighing, inspection, bakery-process, conveyor and palletising-cell OEMs. Each must ship its own branded product, control part of the software release, and lack a visible product-security operations team. Warehouse equipment, water-treatment skids and general industrial controllers are later expansion options. Importers and distributors also come later because their duties and leverage differ.

Reach them through English-language product-security roundtables, adviser introductions and direct product-specific notes to engineering leaders. The note names one observable trigger, such as an approaching release or public vulnerability contact. It does not pitch “the CRA market.” Dutch A2 is insufficient for trust-heavy cold selling into small local factories. The direct hypothesis therefore targets English-capable export manufacturers. A bilingual facilitator is optional for a customer-requested Dutch workshop or manual. That work is separately quoted, customer-funded and carries no referral share; the operation keeps the customer contract. If all ten prospects require Dutch commercial and technical delivery, stop the direct route.

The CRA sale is a paid €7,500 baseline for one family, capped at four weeks. It includes one incident tabletop and does not promise conformity. At closeout, the buyer receives the artefacts even if it declines the retainer. The continuation offer is €3,000 per month for weekly triage, monthly evidence review and quarterly drills. Implementation projects are separately scoped.

The product director may separately buy a €2,500 Data Act module. It maps generated and retrievable data, records counsel-approved release rules and tests one real request. Counsel contracts directly with the manufacturer. The recurring model excludes this module until payment proves recurring demand. All figures are required-price design hurdles, not observed market prices.

The CRA signer is the engineering director who can assign engineering and quality time. A security enthusiast without that authority cannot buy an operating function because each closed item needs internal judgement or code. The dated trigger is the 11 September 2026 reporting start and the need to rehearse before an incident. Urgency earns a meeting, not a purchase order. The baseline reveals whether the buyer values closure or merely wants a gap report.

Year one fails the owner-income gate and should. Three pilot clients cannot pay a senior founder and build the method. Its €27,000 delivery hurdle is transparent: €12,000 of contract support, €9,000 of tooling and €6,000 of secure cloud and backup.

The mature case uses one analyst and two engineers while the founder moves to sales, escalation and product. At 120 productive hours per hire each month, the team has 360 hours. Retainers consume a designed 240 hours monthly: 20 clients at 12 hours each. Ten annual diagnostics average another 50 hours monthly. That is 81% planned utilization, leaving 70 hours for variance and internal improvement. If a client regularly consumes a day each week, the model collapses into a consultancy.

Recurring revenue is €720,000 in the mature model, 86% of revenue. It is earned by changing customer state, not an invented annual report. Gross margin pays delivery staff and software before corporate overhead. The €100,000 founder line is owner compensation before personal tax at replacement cost; the €310,000 surplus is not salary and should mostly remain available for hiring, tax and product investment.

Working capital is the constraint hidden by annual arithmetic. Hire the analyst only after five signed retainers, the first engineer after ten, and the second after fifteen. Bill diagnostics 50% in advance and retainers monthly in advance. The €90,000 reserve hurdle comprises three months of mature delivery cost, or €75,000, plus about six weeks of annual overhead, rounded to €15,000. It covers hiring before capacity is fully used and one late collection cycle. Keep customer-paid laboratory work outside the books. The initial €100,000 is enough only if hiring follows receipts.

From service to company

Customer one is founder work. The asset is not software; it is the first CRA state model: product, version, component, vulnerability, decision, evidence, owner and deadline. A purchased Data Act module adds data fields and requests to those shared identities. Every action records input, decision authority, output and elapsed time. The founder learns where legal judgement ends and implementation begins.

Customer five uses one canonical playbook with product-specific adapters. A trained analyst runs intake, evidence checks and meeting preparation. An engineer handles pipelines and tests; the founder handles risk escalation. At least 60% of routine hours must occur without the founder. Every customer uses the same weekly queue and severity rules. Acquisition begins to repeat through adviser introductions and direct referrals, not five founder friendships.

Customer twenty has a multi-tenant workflow product only where reuse is real: immutable decision history, release evidence, supplier/component links, deadline timers, role-based access and exportable technical-file indexes. Customer code, risk acceptance and legal opinions stay outside or behind strict tenant boundaries. Integrations into Git, issue tracking and document stores are the accumulating asset; so are elapsed-time benchmarks and playbooks. The system does not decide whether a product is legally in scope or whether disclosure would cause serious economic damage.

The four scalability tests are therefore explicit. Acquisition repeats only if two channels each create several paid baselines a year. Delivery is teachable only if analysts close most queue items from written procedures. Value recurs only while releases, vulnerabilities, requests and support states change. The asset accumulates only if integrations and evidence history reduce the next customer's labour. Fail any two and the honest outcome is a good expert practice, not a scalable company.

The incumbent gets a turn

ONEKEY already markets firmware/SBOM analysis, vulnerability monitoring, impact assessment and a compliance workflow for manufacturers, combined with consulting. Cybellum offers a product-security platform spanning SBOMs, risk, vulnerabilities, incident response and regulatory evidence. The EU-funded CRACoWi project states a value of €8.44 million and a term through August 2027. Its wizard is intended to help SMEs, manufacturers, importers and distributors automate CRA assessments and documentation. These are not distant category competitors. They can add service, partner with assessors, lower prices or become the customer's system of record.

Generic substitutes are stronger than their product pages suggest. A competent quality manager can join an SBOM scanner, Jira, SharePoint, counsel and an annual penetration test. An embedded supplier can add the work to an existing development retainer. A CE adviser can own the evidence map. The manufacturer may reasonably prefer internal risk acceptance and customer communication because outsourcing adds another sensitive repository and another party during a 24-hour incident.

Standards will also move the boundary. CEN and CENELEC's 2026 programme says horizontal CRA drafts, vulnerability-handling work and industrial vertical profiles are still being advanced, with key work scheduled for the end of 2026. Clearer standards help delivery but commoditise the map. The operation must own reliable closure across tools, not proprietary interpretations that disappear when guidance improves.

The response is deliberately narrow: serve industrial products where embedded software may arrive as complete images, equipment can have intermittent or no connectivity, and availability constraints can slow updates. Those conditions make a generic workflow a weaker fit. Integrate ONEKEY, Cybellum or open tools when they fit. Never build an SBOM scanner merely to avoid paying a vendor. The defensible margin, if any, sits in cross-functional operation and accumulated customer context.

The Data Act work remains a separately priced implementation module, not a combined recurring product. Its substitutes are counsel, internal product owners, contract repositories and ordinary API integrators. A bilingual facilitator can also become the prime contractor and squeeze the margin. Direct English contracting and customer-funded language support are therefore part of the test, not details to settle after sale.

The capable failure

The company executes well. It recruits industrial-security engineers, builds disciplined integrations, passes customer security reviews and closes queues on time. By late 2027 the first manufacturers have finished their readiness projects. Then the work separates. Quality retains conformity evidence; engineering buys a product-security platform; counsel owns Data Act contracts; a junior internal product-security coordinator runs the weekly meeting. The external operation is renewed only for incident overflow.

Nothing failed operationally. The law created durable tasks but not a durable outsourcing boundary. Better standards reduced interpretation work, product tools automated the mechanical queue, and the remaining decisions were precisely those the manufacturer could not delegate. Revenue settles into diagnostics and occasional remediation. Founder expertise remains valuable, but the twentieth customer requires the same senior attention as the first. This is the strongest objection: capable delivery discovers a finite transition project, not a recurring company.

The first ninety days

  1. Days 1–15: choose only connected food and packaging machinery OEMs. Screen twenty accounts for own-brand products, controlled software releases, field support and no dedicated product-security operation. Secure one product-security lawyer and one machinery/CE adviser to review boundaries.
  2. Days 16–30: conduct ten 45-minute English-language workflow interviews with engineering and quality leaders. Reconstruct the last release, vulnerability and customer security request. Separately ask product directors about the last product-data request. Record who worked, hours lost, deadline, budget owner and paid supplier before pitching.
  3. Days 31–45: offer the €7,500 CRA baseline at one scope and price. Offer the €2,500 Data Act module only to the product director. Maximum pre-evidence spend is €5,000 for counsel review, insurance advice, travel and a disposable prototype. Build no platform and hire nobody.
  4. Days 46–75: deliver at most two paid CRA baselines in each customer's existing tools. Time every task and classify each decision as legal, manufacturer risk, implementation or routine operation. Run the incident clock. Test a product-data path only when its separate module is bought.
  5. Days 76–90: ask each CRA buyer to sign a six-month managed-operation pilot at €3,000 monthly, billed in advance. Passing requires two paid baselines from ten qualified offers and at least one direct retainer. It also requires 60% of forecast recurring work to be analyst or engineer work. Kill after no paid baseline from ten qualified offers. Also kill after two baselines reveal less than one day monthly of recurring external work. If every qualified prospect requires Dutch-led delivery, stop the direct model and reassess it as a partner route.

Verdict

The assumption most able to reverse it is that a small food or packaging machinery OEM will pay an external team monthly to close the CRA queue after the baseline ends.
