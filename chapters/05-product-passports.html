Essay 5 of 7 · Digital product passport / battery-passport integration

Do Not Build Another Passport Platform

On 18 February 2027, a second-life battery integrator placing a repurposed industrial battery over 2 kWh on the EU market does not merely inherit the donor battery's record. It takes responsibility for accurate passport information and must create a new passport linked to the original. The opportunity is not a generic “DPP platform”: Siemens, Circularise, Minespider and Spherity are already there. It is the dirty operational layer between donor records, BMS tests, product data, access rules and one compliant publication route. That layer may earn recurring money—or disappear as soon as the platforms and source systems learn to speak.

The proposition

The first customer is a Benelux company that assembles stationary energy-storage systems from batteries previously used in vehicles or other applications and expects to place its finished industrial batteries on the EU market after 18 February 2027. It is not a cell manufacturer or a vehicle OEM. It buys or receives donor packs or modules, tests and grades them, changes their purpose or configuration, integrates a BMS and enclosure, and becomes the economic operator for the second-life battery.

The buyer is its COO, head of compliance or product-data lead. The purchase event is a dated production batch that crosses the 2027 application date and cannot ship with a spreadsheet standing in for a battery passport. The deliverable is a managed data operation: determine the applicable passport fields with qualified counsel; map each field to a source and responsible person; ingest donor passport references, bill-of-material and test/BMS data; preserve provenance and approvals; generate the QR/identifier handoff; submit required registration metadata; and publish through an existing passport provider chosen with the customer. After launch, the service monitors missing, changed and expiring evidence and handles new units and lifecycle updates.

The business does not host the only copy of the passport, issue product conformity, calculate or certify carbon footprints, verify responsible sourcing, provide battery-safety engineering, or pretend to be the customer's legal adviser. It does not invent a blockchain or proprietary identifier. A battery lawyer interprets scope; testing and conformity bodies assess what requires them; a DPP provider stores and serves the record. The company owns the connectors, validation rules, evidence workflow and operating playbook.

The substitute is a compliance consultant plus spreadsheets and manual uploads, the customer's ERP/PLM team, or the integration/professional-services arm of the selected DPP platform. That last substitute is the serious one. The operation wins only where source data remain too fragmented and lifecycle changes too frequent for “configure once, issue passports” to be true.

What changed

The dated battery obligation is enacted. Article 77 of Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 says that from 18 February 2027 each light-means-of-transport battery, each industrial battery above 2 kWh and each electric-vehicle battery placed on the market or put into service must have an electronic battery passport. It includes model information and information specific to the individual battery. Article 13 separately requires a QR code from the same date; for those covered categories it leads to the passport, and the marking must be visible, legible and indelible on the battery or, where that is not possible or warranted, on packaging and accompanying documents. Article 77(3) says the QR code links to a unique identifier assigned by the placing operator and requires both to comply with the ISO/IEC 15459 family listed there, or equivalent standards.

The narrow second-life chain has an additional trigger. Article 77(7) transfers responsibility for passport accuracy, completeness and currency to the economic operator that places a reused, repurposed or remanufactured battery on the market or puts it into service. It requires a new passport linked to the original passport or passports. Article 13(9) also requires new labels or markings describing the status change. This is why the opening is better than generic passport advice: the operator must join lineage from potentially several donor records to a changed physical product.

Responsibility cannot be outsourced away. Article 77(4) permits written authorisation for another operator to act, but leaves the placing operator responsible for accurate, complete and current information. The same article requires open standards, an interoperable, machine-readable, structured and searchable format, transfer through an open network and no vendor lock-in. Article 78 requires authentication, integrity, role-based access, continued availability after the responsible operator ceases activity, and forbids an authorised processor from reusing passport data beyond the service unless specifically agreed. The contract must therefore distinguish doing the work from owning the legal answer.

The source-data job started before the passport date. Since 18 August 2024, stationary battery energy-storage systems, LMT batteries and EV batteries must hold current state-of-health and expected-lifetime parameters in the BMS. Purchasers and authorised third parties receive read-only access for purposes including valuation and repurposing, subject to manufacturer intellectual-property rights. That is a legal access path, not proof that old donor packs expose clean APIs or that the parameters are comparable. A paid diagnostic must test actual extraction.

Some common architecture is now real. The Commission's DPP Registry went live on 20 July 2026 and provides a testing environment. The registry stores identifiers, registration data and high-level metadata rather than the full passport; product data remain with the economic operator or its service provider in a decentralised architecture. The Commission's August 2026 battery guidance assembles 71 data points and marks their applicability by battery category, while warning that the guidance creates no new legal requirements and is not an authoritative interpretation.

Important pieces are still unsettled on 1 September 2026. The Commission's published implementation timeline schedules the battery access-rights implementing act for Q4 2026, the remaining two DPP standards for September 2026, and delegated requirements for DPP service providers in 2027. Build schemas and adapters now; do not freeze a proprietary access model or promise final conformance before those acts arrive.

Do not merge this with the broader ESPR story. Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 is a framework under which product-specific delegated acts define passport requirements, and the Commission says economic operators receive at least an 18-month transition after an ESPR delegated act. Its working plan prioritises groups such as textiles, furniture, tyres, mattresses, iron and steel, and aluminium. Those priorities and indicative adoption dates are not present-day passport obligations for every item in those sectors. Batteries have their own enacted regulation and date. Selling “ESPR compliance for all products in 2027” would be false.

Battery due diligence is separate again. Regulation (EU) 2025/1561 moved the Article 48 due-diligence start to 18 August 2027. Chapter VII also excludes operators below its €40 million turnover threshold (unless their consolidated group exceeds it) and excludes batteries already marketed before reuse/repurposing from that chapter's scope. Those rules do not move the passport date. This offer tracks a due-diligence document only when it is a required passport input; it does not sell the passport as a complete due-diligence programme.

Law creates the deadline, not the budget. The live registry, 71-point guidance and second-life responsibility show a real implementation job. They do not show that a small integrator will pay an external operator rather than its existing quality engineer or platform vendor. That remains the paid-test question.

The first sale

Build the first list from ten explicit prospect types: an EV-pack second-life integrator; bus-battery repurposer; forklift-battery remanufacturer; home-to-commercial storage upgrader; containerised BESS assembler using recovered modules; marine auxiliary-storage integrator; telecom backup-power remanufacturer; industrial UPS battery rebuilder; recycler launching a graded second-life product; and a battery leasing operator that becomes the placing economic operator after remanufacture. Screen out firms whose 2027 products are below 2 kWh, remain prototypes, or are placed by an OEM that retains all passport responsibility.

The founder should approach through one named category of domain partner: a battery conformity/safety consultancy that will sign its own interpretation, not a generic sustainability agency. Add one passport-platform solutions partner so the diagnostic tests a real publication route. The introduction asks for a specific production event: “Which finished battery will you place on the market after 18 February, who is the economic operator, and can you trace ten planned units back to donor passports, tests and BMS records?” A company without a dated batch is a research interview, not a prospect.

The first offer is a six-week €12,000 passport-readiness sprint, a proposed test price. It covers one finished product family and up to ten test units. Outputs are a counsel-reviewed applicability memo; a field-by-field responsibility and evidence matrix; lineage mapping to original passport identifiers; extraction of one authorised BMS/test dataset; role/access design marked “provisional” where the implementing act is pending; ten test-environment passport payloads; a provider comparison; and a fixed production quote. It excludes legal opinions beyond the scoped memo, conformity assessment and new source-data creation. Fifty per cent is paid before work.

The COO or compliance lead signs. Operations, engineering, quality and IT each name one owner. The customer contracts directly with the passport host, so licence cost and continuity rights remain visible and there is no hidden reseller lock-in. English should work for cross-border suppliers and technical teams, but local production records, regulator contact and shop-floor evidence may be Dutch or German. At A2 Dutch the founder must budget professional translation and let the qualified local partner lead regulatory conversations.

The required production price is €30,000 for integration/onboarding, then €3,500 per month for managed evidence and change operations plus €12 per newly issued or materially updated passport event. These are hurdles, not market facts. The platform licence, identifier issuance, lab work and legal opinions are billed directly to the customer. If a ten-unit sprint cannot justify that next-stage order, do not assume thousands of future passports will.

Does the money work?

A high-value stationary system may contain fewer passported units than a vehicle programme. Per-passport fees cannot carry this company. The retainer pays for the changing state: supplier evidence arrives, tests are rerun, a donor lineage is corrected, roles change, a unit is updated, a standard or data rule changes, or an authority asks for proof. The event fee disciplines volume; the operating retainer funds readiness.

The model clears the owner-income target and retains cash only because customers pay a substantial retainer and platform licences stay out of revenue. If the managed work settles into one annual CSV upload, the retainer will be competed away. At €12 per event alone, 4,000 events produce €48,000: less than one engineer. Conversely, if every passport needs two hours of manual source chasing, 4,000 events consume roughly four full-time working years before review and support. The acceptable process must make the typical event mostly automatic and reserve human time for exceptions.

Working capital deserves restraint. Bill sprints 50% upfront, onboarding 40/40/20 against evidence-map, test and production milestones, and retainers quarterly in advance. Keep provider licences on the customer's contract and do not finance laboratory work. The first 90 days need at most €25,000; a production company should preserve roughly €90,000 for six months of a small team's payroll and specialist commitments. That is almost the reader's full capital allowance, which is another reason not to build hosting infrastructure before payment.

Track economics by data object, not by customer logo: automated versus manual fields, evidence rejection rate, minutes per passport event, supplier follow-up days, schema-change rework, and exceptions requiring counsel. A customer with five product variants and one disciplined ERP may be cheaper than a one-product customer with ten donor sources. Price the data topology found in the sprint, not nominal passport count.

From service to company

Customer one is an evidence archaeology project. The founder and battery adviser decide whether the finished item is in scope, trace ten units to donor sources, discover which BMS parameters can actually be read, reconcile serial numbers and document every unresolved interpretation. Test payloads go to the Commission's registry environment and one selected provider. Every manual choice becomes a versioned rule with its source and approver.

Customer five should use a canonical internal schema with source provenance, evidence status, role/access class and effective date on each field. Connectors pull from two or three common ERP/PLM formats, a test bench export and a BMS gateway. Provider adapters translate that record into Siemens, Circularise, Spherity, Minespider or another selected endpoint without making one of them the internal truth. A trained integration engineer runs onboarding; the compliance partner reviews only scope and exceptions.

Customer twenty is a managed exception operation. Supplier reminders, validation, lineage checks, identifier registration, change approval and publication are automated. Humans resolve missing donor links, conflicting measurements, status changes and questions whose legal treatment has moved. The recurring value comes from real state changes, not an invented annual report. Acquisition repeats through battery compliance firms, test laboratories, recycling networks and DPP implementation partners, with one rule: no partner may both own the customer and silently substitute the company at renewal.

The accumulating asset is a regression-tested mapping library: legal data point to canonical field to source evidence to provider payload; fixtures for registry/provider conformance; supplier evidence templates; provenance and access-control patterns; and measured failure modes from real onboarding. It is not a database of customer battery secrets. Contracts should leave raw and transformed customer data with the customer, guarantee full export, restrict cross-customer reuse, and permit reuse only of generic mappings, code, empty templates and de-identified operational metrics.

Verification must be explicit. The system can validate presence, type, range, signature, lineage and approval. It cannot prove that a supplier's composition declaration is true or that a laboratory test was competent. Each field records source, issuer, date, transformation and approval; derived values preserve their inputs and algorithm version. Public, authority-only and legitimate-interest views are generated from one controlled record, not copied into three spreadsheets. Where access rules remain pending, the implementation uses a reversible policy table and a test credential—not a hard-coded interpretation.

The incumbent gets a turn

There is no platform gap. Siemens offers a Battery Passport REST API with user and passport management, including create, update and retrieve operations. Circularise markets model- and individual-battery data, QR access and permissions in one platform. Minespider markets EU battery-passport tooling and an open API. Spherity markets templates, APIs, secure access and a fully white-label-ready product. Their claims of compliance are vendor claims; only the responsible economic operator carries the legal duty. But their existence means a new generic passport host starts behind on product, standards work, security evidence and integrations.

Spherity explicitly invites consultancies and integrators to resell or white-label its software. That is both route and threat. Use such a platform to reach production quickly, but keep the customer contract, canonical export and provider-neutral test suite. Siemens can bundle its passport into an industrial digital stack; Circularise and Minespider can add onboarding services; an ERP vendor can generate the payload at source. The proposed company keeps margin only if it repeatedly solves evidence and change operations those products cannot economically standardise.

Interoperability is not a slogan here. The Regulation itself requires open, transferable data without vendor lock-in, and the EU registry indexes rather than stores the full record. A provider that blocks export is a compliance risk as well as a commercial one. Yet formal interoperability can commoditise the connector. The more successful the standards become, the less anyone should pay merely to translate JSON. Margin must migrate toward data provenance, supplier follow-up, exception handling and accountable operations.

The capable failure

The company chooses the right niche, partners with a competent battery lawyer, ships ten valid test passports, builds clean connectors and wins six second-life integrators. Then the standards and access act settle well. Donor OEMs expose reliable passport links; test-equipment vendors export the canonical fields; the leading DPP providers add second-life templates, lineage and registry submission; customers' ERP integrators map the remaining data during ordinary implementation.

The founders have done nothing wrong. Their interoperability work helped make the problem routine. But each customer places relatively few high-value stationary systems, lifecycle updates are rare, and the supposedly recurring evidence queue shrinks after onboarding. Procurement compares a €42,000 annual managed retainer with a platform module and twenty days of internal quality-engineer time. The platform vendor absorbs the connector into its licence; the battery adviser keeps the occasional interpretation. This company is squeezed out of the middle by the standardisation it correctly anticipated. It can survive as project integration, but not reach the recurring mature-year model.

The first ninety days

  1. Days 1–12: retain an EU battery-regulation lawyer or conformity specialist for a capped €4,000 memo on the selected second-life scenario: product category, placing operator, Article 77/78 duties, Article 45 boundary and open implementation questions. Do not generalise it to all batteries.
  2. Days 1–25: interview ten in-scope prospect types. Require a dated 2027 production family, estimated unit count, donor-source map, current system of record and named budget owner. Record whether the buyer already has a DPP platform or ERP programme.
  3. Days 15–35: select one existing DPP provider and one fallback after testing export, API access, lineage, role controls, registry workflow, price transparency and exit. Use the Commission registry testing environment; do not build a production host.
  4. Days 25–55: build a canonical evidence record and ten synthetic or customer-authorised test passports. Exercise one original-to-new lineage, one BMS/test import, public/restricted views and a full export. Mark pending legal rules in configuration.
  5. Days 35–75: offer at least five €12,000 readiness sprints, 50% upfront, with the production prices stated before sale. A free workshop does not count.
  6. Days 60–90: deliver one paid sprint and ask for the €30,000 onboarding plus €3,500 monthly managed-data order. Cap all pre-evidence cash at €25,000, including legal advice, provider sandbox, security review, travel and translation.

Pass: one in-scope operator pays for the sprint, supplies authorised donor/BMS/test evidence, completes ten linked test records and signs a production order or dated conditional order at the stated price level. The evidence map must show at least one recurring change process beyond initial issuance. Kill: counsel finds the selected 2027 product outside the obligation; no paid sprint after five scoped offers; original passport/BMS data cannot be obtained under workable rights; or customers will buy onboarding but none can identify enough recurring state change to support the retainer. A standards delay is a reason to keep mappings reversible, not to invent a different obligation.

Verdict

TEST THROUGH A PARTNER

The date and second-life responsibility justify a paid test now, but only beside a battery-regulation specialist and on top of an existing DPP provider. The single assumption that would most change the verdict is that small second-life operators experience enough continuing evidence and lifecycle work to pay roughly €42,000 a year after initial integration. If the readiness sprint finds a one-time mapping followed by rare updates, this is consultancy, not a scalable second engine.
