Essay 7 of 7 · EUDI Wallet Acceptance for Regulated Onboarding

A deadline opens the interface. It does not reserve the margin.

European law will make wallet acceptance mandatory for some public services and some non-small private organisations, but not for every company that checks an identity. A mid-size pre-employment screener may still be pushed by clients to accept verified identity and qualification credentials. The proposed company supplies the verifier, prepares the customer's registration and keeps trust and policy current. Transaction evidence stays under customer control. The opening is dated; the margin is not. Signicat, Datakeeper, Yivi, Ver.iD and Sphereon already cover parts of the path. The decision is whether a screening partner will pay for an independent operating layer before a broad identity vendor makes wallet acceptance one more included connector.

The proposition

The first buyer is a mid-size Netherlands-based pre-employment screening provider. It checks identity, education or professional evidence for employers, but does not maintain its own digital-identity protocol team. Its product or operations director owns the budget; the screener's legal entity signs. The purchase event is an enterprise client asking for a wallet option in a tender, or a production wallet and useful credential becoming available before the screener has a safe acceptance path.

The offer is a verifier-as-a-service plus relying-party operation for one screening workflow. It creates the wallet request, receives the presentation, validates holder binding, signatures, issuer trust and credential status, and returns a narrow result to the screening case system. It prepares registration data, manages access-certificate changes, tests supported wallets and monitors failed flows. It writes the decision record directly to a customer-controlled evidence store.

The customer still decides what evidence satisfies a check, which issuers it trusts, which attributes are necessary, how long results are retained and when a human reviews an exception. Counsel determines the legal basis, the strong-authentication duty and the data-protection boundary. The service verifies cryptographic and policy conditions; it does not promise that every issuing source is accurate or that a credential proves suitability for employment.

This business refuses to issue wallets, become a general identity provider, conduct the underlying background investigation or sell directly to the screener's employer clients. It also refuses a universal “accept every credential” claim. The first boundary is person-identification data plus one education or professional credential used inside an existing screening case. Passport-image checking and the current manual route remain available as fallback.

The substitute is a project in the screener's own engineering team, using an open-source verifier and its existing identity vendor. A second substitute is to wait until the incumbent screening platform exposes a wallet button. The entrant wins only if registration, trust changes, evidence and exception handling remain costly after the first connection. A clean API alone is ordinary software integration.

What changed

Regulation (EU) 2024/1183 is enacted law. It requires each Member State to provide at least one certified wallet within 24 months after the relevant implementation and certification acts entered into force. Use remains voluntary, and people who do not use a wallet must retain access through existing identification and authentication means.

The protocol and interface act, Regulation (EU) 2024/2982, and the certification act, Regulation (EU) 2024/2981, were published on 4 December 2024. Each entered into force twenty days after publication. Both relevant act families therefore entered into force on 24 December 2024. Reading that date with the Regulation's 24- and 36-month clocks gives working dates of 24 December 2026 for Member State provision and 24 December 2027 for covered private acceptance. This is direct date arithmetic, not a separately announced Dutch launch date.

The acceptance obligation has three different edges. A public body must accept a conforming wallet where its Member State requires electronic identification and authentication for an online service. A private relying party must accept one only if it is not a micro or small enterprise and law or contract already requires strong user authentication for online identification. Very large online platforms that require user authentication also have an acceptance duty, on the user's voluntary request and for minimum necessary data.

Transport, energy, banking, financial services, social security, health, drinking water, postal services, digital infrastructure, education and telecommunications appear as examples in the private rule. Pre-employment screening, housing and notarial work are not named. A screener is not automatically obliged merely because it verifies identity. Whether a particular workflow meets the legal or contractual strong-authentication test needs counsel and the underlying contract.

Registration creates a separate operating surface. Any relying party intending to use wallets for a digital public or private service must register where it is established. It must state its identity, contact details, intended use and requested data; request no other data; report changes without delay; identify itself to the user; and validate the requested credentials. An intermediary acting for a relying party is itself treated as a relying party and may not store transaction-content data.

Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/848 applies from 24 December 2026. It requires national registers, published registration policies, human- and machine-readable access, and at least one authorised access-certificate authority. A separate relying-party registration certificate is optional for a Member State. Registrars must verify application information against supporting documents or official records and verify entitlement under Annex III. Automation is required only where possible. Registered parties must keep their information accurate and current.

That is not yet a complete Dutch production path. The Dutch government's current page says the public NL Wallet is still being developed and will arrive later because the national implementation act is still being prepared. It expects that act to enter public consultation near the end of 2026. The government repository has nevertheless published pre-release artefacts. Version 0.5.0, dated 27 March 2026, includes issuance and verification servers plus a web integration helper.

The European technical baseline is moving too. The Commission records that Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/1731 amended the earlier acts for protocols, trust, person-identification data and core wallet functions. That does not erase the statutory clock. It does make a hard-coded verifier more fragile and strengthens the case for an adapter and conformance-test operation.

Timeline slippage is a commercial risk even when the legal dates remain unchanged. If a production Dutch wallet, register or useful qualification credential arrives six months later than a buyer plans, the integration budget can pause. The company must not hire against a compliance date when its target is driven by client pressure rather than a direct obligation.

None of this establishes budget. The NL Wallet repository includes sharing a diploma during a job application as an example. It does not establish the rest of a screening workflow. No primary source found here shows a mid-size Dutch screener paying an independent operator to accept EUDI credentials. Regulation creates a possible purchase event. Only a paid diagnostic can distinguish it from a backlog item assigned to the existing identity supplier.

The first sale

Build a ten-account list inside one segment. Include general employment, education, healthcare staffing, regulated-role and international-hire screeners. Add an industrial-licence checker, contractor-compliance platform, temporary-workforce provider, executive due-diligence firm and screening API sold through HR software.

Each account must already receive identity or qualification evidence digitally, sell to more than one employer, and control an integration point in its case system. Exclude banks and telecom operators with identity teams. Exclude a direct sale to a housing corporation or notary until the company can prove the workflow with a screening partner. Those buyers add Dutch domain rules before the verifier boundary is learned.

The opening question is not “Are you ready for eIDAS 2?” Reconstruct the last client tender that changed an evidence requirement. Ask who owns the identity vendor, which credential would replace which document, how exceptions are cleared, and whether the client would accept a wallet-derived result. Then show the exact legal boundary: direct duty, client-contract pressure or neither.

The first product is a four-week €8,000 paid acceptance diagnostic, a required-price hypothesis. It covers one workflow and one case-system sandbox. Deliverables are an obligation memo reviewed by counsel, attribute-minimisation map, trust and credential policy, registration-data pack, working test presentation, exception route, evidence-store design, fallback route and production quote. It does not promise access to a Dutch production register before that route exists.

The production test is a €15,000 onboarding fee plus €2,000 per month, also a design hurdle rather than an observed market price. Transaction volume above an agreed band is separately priced only after real load is known. The retainer pays for certificate and registration changes, wallet compatibility, trust and status updates, failure triage, customer-run evidence-export tests and quarterly recovery tests.

The screening partner leads Dutch employer conversations and remains accountable for the screening conclusion. The founder can sell and deliver the technical layer in English, but A2 Dutch is insufficient for legal-purpose workshops or Dutch exception notices. Budget a bilingual identity lawyer and require the partner to own Dutch client discovery. If buyers demand founder-led Dutch sales and policy interpretation, the route fails the reader's constraint.

Year one deliberately misses the owner-income gate. It buys evidence and a reusable adapter, not a salary illusion. The mature design can pay €100,000 owner compensation before personal tax and retain €285,000 before corporation tax. That surplus must fund protocol changes, security assurance, hiring and a bad quarter; it is not all distributable income.

Recurring revenue is €600,000, or 77% of mature revenue, from twenty average active paying screening-provider accounts. Opening and closing accounts are both twenty; eight additions replace eight churned accounts.

One workload rule drives the cost. After account five, onboarding and routine operation together may consume at most six supplier person-days per active account annually. Mature account delivery is therefore 20 × 6 = 120 days. Eight four-day diagnostics add 32 days, and specialist review adds 12. At the €800 loaded-day hurdle plus €30,000 cloud and support, delivery cost rounds to €161,000. Year one similarly rounds 38 delivery days plus €10,000 infrastructure to €40,000.

An implementation-and-operations lead plus flexible analyst and security cover provides a 300-day delivery pool. Reserve 40 days for releases and incidents, 32 for diagnostics and 12 for specialist review. The remaining 216 days support at most 36 active accounts at the six-day ceiling. Founder replacement cost is separate.

Price support measures buyer value, not supplier busyness. At renewal, the screener must document avoided internal labour or tender and contract risk sufficient to approve the €24,000 pilot price or €30,000 mature price. A quiet, automated month can still be valuable. A labour-heavy account that exceeds six delivery days fails the scalable model.

Working capital remains material despite no hardware. The design requires a €75,000 reserve before the second full-time hire, covering roughly three months of payroll, contractors, cloud and insurance while receivables lag. Diagnostics are billed 50% in advance, onboarding 50% in advance, and retainers quarterly in advance. Do not spend the reader's full €100,000 before repeatable payment.

The model breaks first on price, not compute. If a broad identity vendor includes wallet acceptance inside an existing contract, a screening partner compares €30,000 annual recurring cost with zero visible incremental licence cost. The entrant must then prove that registration custody, policy evidence and cross-wallet recovery avoid enough client-specific labour or contract risk to remain a separate line item.

From service to company

Customer one is a controlled integration project. The founder maps one screening decision to the minimum attributes, deploys the verifier beside the customer's sandbox, records every trust decision and writes the fallback route. The assets are a threat model, policy schema, evidence format and a precise list of what still requires counsel or screening judgement.

Customer five uses a versioned adapter, not five forks. An implementation engineer connects the case system. An analyst prepares registration changes, runs conformance cases and investigates failures from a written queue. The founder handles unusual trust and liability decisions. At least 65% of delivery hours must be performed without the founder, and two partners must reach the same acceptance result from the same policy fixture.

Customer twenty means twenty paying screening-provider accounts introduced through three to five HR-technology or advisory channel partners. Each screener's legal entity signs the service order and data terms, receives the invoice and registers as the relying party. The operator prepares changes and runs the verifier as its intermediary. The screener controls purpose, policy, evidence retention and employer or applicant exceptions.

The channel partner introduces the account, leads Dutch commercial discovery and receives 15% of onboarding and recurring fees. It does not enter the wallet flow or receive transaction evidence. If a channel partner or employer requests wallet data for its own service, this topology no longer applies. Counsel must then determine its relying-party and intermediary position before launch.

Article 5b(10) deems the operator, as intermediary, a relying party and prohibits it from storing transaction-content data. The conservative architecture sends disclosed attributes, verification results, issuer and credential references, transaction timestamps and case-level errors directly to the screener's customer-controlled store. The operator keeps only release records, certificate tasks, aggregate service metrics, synthetic conformance fixtures and non-transactional playbooks.

Dutch counsel must decide whether correlation identifiers, customer policy-version references or support logs count as transaction content. Until that opinion and the data terms exist, the operator stores none of them. Employer and applicant exceptions remain in the screener's case system.

Acquisition repeats only if channel partners introduce a fixed acceptance package in client tenders. Delivery is teachable only if an analyst completes most registration and test work from playbooks. The accumulating company asset is the conformance corpus, reusable case-system adapters, synthetic test credentials, failure taxonomy and release tooling. It is not a company-owned history of applicant transactions.

The founder bottleneck is legal-technical translation. A verifier can reject a bad signature automatically; it cannot decide whether a nursing qualification is sufficient, whether identity matching is proportionate or whether a new attribute has lawful purpose. Those remain partner and counsel decisions. If every policy change returns to the founder, the result is a boutique identity consultancy.

The dated interface does not, by itself, make this more than “still software.” The possible second engine is the operating boundary around registration, policy, conformance and recovery. If buyers treat those as implementation chores, the interface produces a temporary software project, not a scalable company.

The incumbent gets a turn

Signicat markets one integration to 27-plus wallets and more than 35 electronic identities, plus help with assessment, technical integration and relying-party registration. That is nearly the proposed surface, attached to a larger identity platform and trust-service position. Its obvious response is to include EUDI acceptance in an existing contract.

Datakeeper already markets identity, income and financial-data sharing for real estate and social housing. Its developer documentation exposes a verifier API with QR initiation, webhooks and multi-tenant use by service providers. For housing, rental and sharing platforms, this is an operating substitute rather than a future logo on a competitor slide.

Yivi already supplies a standard wallet-to-verifier presentation flow: a verifier requests credentials, the wallet returns them, and the verifier reads the disclosed claims. It implements this through OpenID4VP and SD-JWT credentials. Ver.iD lists Datakeeper, Digidentity and Yivi integrations as production, but places EUDI Wallet and NL Wallet integrations in testing. Those are vendor status labels, not independent assurance, but they show how quickly one integration layer can span old and new wallets.

Sphereon markets verification, trust resolution, policy enforcement, status checking and audit evidence for EUDI credentials. Again, that is marketing evidence of product scope, not proof of outcomes. Together these firms can sell the protocol, the adjacent identity checks and the procurement comfort. The entrant offers less breadth and carries new-vendor security review.

The internal substitute also improves. The Dutch repository already publishes pre-release verification-server binaries and a web helper. A capable enterprise developer can deploy that path and keep policy in the case system. The national registration process is required to be electronic, easy to use and automated where possible. If it works as intended, “we manage your registration” may be a modest administrative task rather than recurring value.

The response is not another proprietary verifier. Use the public components where sound, test several wallets, and sell custody of the acceptance outcome: declared purpose matches requested data; certificates are valid; trust and status are evaluated; failures recover; evidence reaches the screening case; and fallback remains usable. Even this is vulnerable to bundling. The partner must buy measurable operating assurance, not protocol access.

The capable failure

The company executes well. It joins the Dutch test environment, passes security review, integrates three screening systems and maintains a disciplined conformance suite. Wallets arrive and applicants use them. The national register is straightforward, certificates rotate predictably, and the common protocols settle. Precisely because the ecosystem works, the recurring exception load is small.

At the same time, each screener's incumbent identity provider adds wallet presentations to its existing API at no incremental licence charge. The provider already supplies document checks, fraud signals, service support and procurement assurances. Employer clients accept its evidence format. Screeners keep the entrant only for migration and unusual credentials, then cancel the retainer after twelve months.

Nothing here is poor execution. Standardisation removes bespoke work; good national infrastructure removes registration friction; bundling moves the remaining margin to vendors with an installed identity contract. The company has built a useful adapter and delivered the transition responsibly. It has not created a durable operating boundary. Revenue returns to projects, and the twentieth customer needs another enterprise sale for less work than the subscription price must justify.

The first ninety days

  1. Days 1–15: retain a Dutch digital-identity lawyer for a fixed €2,500 review. Produce a two-page test separating direct Article 5f duty, contractual pressure and voluntary adoption. Apply it to ten screening accounts without claiming that the segment is automatically obliged.
  2. Days 10–30: interview ten product or operations directors and five employer clients introduced by them. Reconstruct one tender, one failed document check and one qualification-verification exception. Ask what the incumbent identity provider has committed, who owns budget and which evidence can be replaced.
  3. Days 20–45: deploy the Dutch pre-release verifier only in a disposable test environment. Add one alternative test wallet, a minimal policy fixture, synthetic credentials and an export to a mock screening case. Spend at most €2,500 on cloud, test support and security review.
  4. Days 31–60: offer the same €8,000 diagnostic to eight qualified prospects. Require 50% in advance, one named workflow, access to a sandbox and the participation of compliance, product and the employer-facing owner. Free technical demonstrations do not count.
  5. Days 46–80: deliver no more than two diagnostics. Time registration preparation, integration, policy decisions, test maintenance and exception handling separately. Put the €15,000 onboarding and €2,000 monthly production price in the closeout proposal.
  6. Days 81–90: maximum total pre-evidence spend is €7,500, including legal review, Dutch support, travel, cloud and test security. No employee, certification claim or general wallet platform is funded before payment.

Pass: two of eight qualified offers become paid €8,000 diagnostics, and at least one buyer signs the €15,000 onboarding plus a six-month €2,000 monthly pilot. Its budget owner must document avoided internal labour or tender and contract risk sufficient to support a €24,000–€30,000 annual recurring price. Delivery must remain below six supplier days per active account annually. Kill: no paid diagnostic after eight offers, every incumbent includes acceptance without separate budget, or neither delivered diagnostic produces that documented value case. In a kill state, take premium integration projects only if consultancy is acceptable.

Verdict

TEST THROUGH A PARTNER

The single assumption that would most change this verdict is that a pre-employment screening partner will pay a separate recurring price for registration, policy evidence and wallet compatibility after its incumbent identity provider exposes EUDI acceptance.
